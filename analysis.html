Party for Socialism and Liberation · Massachusetts

From electoral advance to durable organization

PSL’s vote total grew 4.59× and its vote share grew 5.86× from Claudia De La Cruz in 2024 to Joe Tache in 2026. The result shows a durable but broadening electoral geography—not, by itself, an equivalent growth in socialist organization.

Race clarification: the supplied official file places Andrea James and Kayla Kittredge in the 2026 governor’s race, not the Senate race. Joe Tache ran for U.S. Senate. Comparisons with James therefore cross offices.

What “growth” means here

The evidence supports “PSL electoral support grew 4.59-fold and its vote share grew 5.86-fold.” It does not support “socialist consciousness or organization grew 5.86-fold.” Membership, retention, leadership, and mass-activity data are needed for that claim.

Up from zero in 2024. Precincts above 2% increased from 41 to 1,137.

97

Municipalities above 2%

Also up from zero. Twenty-nine municipalities exceeded 3%, compared with none in 2024.

125 → 33

Zero-PSL precincts

Of 125 precincts with no PSL vote in 2024, 96 recorded at least one Tache vote in 2026.

Geographic persistence

Pearson r = 0.727 · weighted r = 0.792

Across 2,382 matched precincts, De La Cruz and Tache support occupies similar geography. Spearman’s rank correlation is 0.645. A municipal cluster bootstrap puts the weighted correlation’s 95% interval at 0.645–0.834.

After controlling for Harris 2024 strength, the weighted partial correlation remains 0.730.

Turnout robustness

Growth occurred in every turnout quintile

The correlation between PSL share growth and precinct turnout retention is only r = −0.101. Lower turnout cannot explain away the result.

The ten largest PSL-vote municipalities supplied 39.14% of the vote in 2024 but 33.09% in 2026: support became less concentrated even as strongholds deepened.

02 · Adjacent electorates

A distinct PSL geography, not an undifferentiated “left vote”

The Green and Democratic primary results help locate sympathetic or dissatisfied electorates, but aggregate returns cannot establish individual vote transfers or political agreement.

James and Stein

Moderate overlap, different coalitions

Tache and James correlate at r = 0.519 when weighted by turnout. Stein 2024 and James 2026 correlate at only 0.208, far below PSL’s 0.792 continuity.

James is essentially uncorrelated with Harris strength (r = −0.067), while Tache is positively correlated (r = 0.475). PSL is more rooted in Democratic/progressive geography; James reached a broader protest electorate.

Tache outpolled James in only 16 of 351 municipalities, but those included Boston, Somerville, Cambridge, Northampton, Amherst, Salem, Chelsea, and Medford.

Markey and Moulton

Affinity with Markey-primary geography

Tache’s precinct share correlates with Markey’s two-party primary share at r = 0.564, or 0.618 weighted. Controlling for Harris 2024 leaves a weighted partial correlation of 0.567.

This identifies a potentially receptive progressive geography. It does not show that individual Markey primary voters transferred to Tache, particularly because the primary electorate was smaller and self-selected.

Do not add the two 2026 races and call it a unique bloc

The summed PSL/Green vote totals rose from 39,434 (1.123%) in 2024 to 149,048 (5.421%) in 2026. PSL’s share of those summed totals increased from 32.7% to 39.6%. But Senate and governor votes on the same ballots are not separate people, and the 2024 and 2026 offices differ. These are candidate-total comparisons, not a deduplicated coalition count.

Consolidate the strongest bases

Allston-Brighton: Ward 21 reached 10.50%; Ward 21 Precinct 4 reached 18.10%. This is the clearest place to turn electoral support into durable neighborhood organization.

Somerville: every ward fell between 6.60% and 8.72%, led by Ward 2 at 8.72%. Strength is broad, not dependent on one outlier.

Cambridge: Wards 3, 5, and 4 reached 6.45%, 5.93%, and 5.79%.

Build from local peaks; diagnose weaker penetration

Lowell: Wards 4 and 7 reached 6.33% and 5.82%. Worcester: Wards 10, 8, 4, and 3 reached 5.91%, 4.85%, 4.52%, and 4.23%.

Springfield: Wards 3 and 6 led at 3.27% and 3.09%. James’s stronger 4.614% indicates a wider alternative vote that did not identify with PSL to the same degree.

Cape Cod: only Wellfleet (2.364%) and Provincetown (2.136%) exceeded the statewide PSL share. Falmouth and Barnstable supplied the most votes, 294 and 321. This suggests branch repair and local base-building rather than chasing percentages alone.

Three distinctions that prevent misreading the model
  • 25–34 is not simply “student” or 18–24 support. It is the clearest age relationship, but the data do not identify students, renters, workers, or individual voters.
  • Lower-income support is not uniquely socialist. James’s weighted correlation with log income was an even stronger −0.579. Economic distress does not automatically imply socialist consciousness.
  • Simple racial correlations are not group preferences. After full controls, Hispanic and Asian coefficients were near zero while the Black coefficient slightly reversed. Neighborhood composition is not individual voting behavior.

06 · Elections and movement

Reject both abstention and electoralism

Lenin’s argument is not that elections are useless, nor that electing Communists is itself the road to revolution. Elections are one form of mass engagement whose value must be judged by what they help build beyond parliament.

Communists should not simply strive to “get seats” in parliament, but should help people think and draw the masses into struggle, using the electoral machinery established by the bourgeoisie. Paraphrase with a short quotation from Lenin, “Left-Wing” Communism: an Infantile Disorder, chapter 10.

  1. Turn strongholds into organizing bases

    Allston-Brighton, Somerville, Cambridge, Roxbury, Lowell, and Worcester should not merely receive higher vote targets. Use them for recurring neighborhood organization, political education, tenant and labor activity, and leadership development.

  2. Expand through satellites before creating branches

    Existing branches should adopt nearby opportunity clusters and build durable local committees. A new branch is justified by sustained local leadership and activity, not vote share alone.

  3. Maintain independence while engaging wider electorates

    The Markey and Green relationships locate potentially sympathetic or dissatisfied audiences. They do not justify dissolving PSL into progressivism or treating every protest voter as a socialist. Joint activity around concrete issues should preserve independent organization and political critique.

  4. Prioritize mass experience over campaign-only contact

    Election contacts need year-round routes into local struggles, meetings, and responsibility. A voter file without that progression is an electoral asset, not yet a movement.

Participation is a means of mass education

In chapter 7, Lenin calls participation obligatory where masses still engage with bourgeois institutions. The task is to meet that reality without mistaking the vanguard’s consciousness for that of the whole class.

Tactical flexibility must raise capacity

In chapter 8, compromises and shifts in tactics are judged by whether they raise class consciousness, revolutionary spirit, and the ability to fight and win—not by abstract purity.

Measure the next campaign against a real baseline

For the next comparable election, use 2026 as the baseline. Compare organized precincts with matched precincts having similar prior PSL support, Democratic strength, turnout, age, and income. A preregistered difference-in-differences analysis would estimate organizing impact better than raw vote changes.

The immediate electoral baseline is 59,097 votes, 2.149%, 195 precincts above 5%, and 1,137 above 2%. The first organizational target should be systematic follow-up in high-support precincts within selected branch turf, followed by the wider 2% tier.

07 · Reproducibility

Methods, limitations, and source files

All 9,573 precinct CSV rows passed component-to-total validation. Official statewide totals, branch/state sums, and output invariants were independently reconciled.

Full numerical results · JSON Reproducible analysis · Python Explore the interactive map

Re-run the analysis with python3 analyze_psl_growth.py. The script uses only the Python standard library. A full refresh requires internet access for Census Reporter income estimates and Boston neighborhood boundaries.

Statistical approach

  • 2,393 precinct keys matched across races; 2,382 have nonzero ballots in both general elections.
  • Pearson and Spearman correlations, turnout weighting, partial correlations, weighted least-squares regression, and municipal cluster bootstraps.
  • Tract rows require at least 200 ballots in 2026 and valid income. VTDs are assigned to tracts by an interior point; approximately 93% of ballots are covered.
  • Opportunity scores combine vote scale, persistence, and residuals from a weighted 2024 PSL/Stein/Harris baseline model.

Limits on interpretation

  • Vote counts are complete results, not survey samples. Bootstrap intervals describe geographic stability, not uncertainty in official totals.
  • Different offices and campaign contexts limit 2024–2026 comparisons. No individual voter transfers can be inferred.
  • Census findings are ecological: tract composition cannot identify an individual’s race, income, class position, age, or motivation.
  • Race/age use ACS 2022 and income uses ACS 2024. No occupation, union, renter, membership, or canvassing-exposure data are available.
  • Roxbury and Dorchester estimates depend on mapped VTD points and nonofficial planning boundaries.

Sources

  • Supplied Massachusetts PD43+ precinct files: 2024 president, 2026 U.S. Senate, 2026 governor, and 2026 Democratic Senate primary.
  • Official race pages: 2026 U.S. Senate and 2026 governor.
  • Local ACS 2022 tract race and age files; Census Reporter B19013 median household income, ACS 2024 five-year.
  • Boston Planning Department neighborhood boundaries, used only for approximate Roxbury and Dorchester estimates.
  • Lenin, “Left-Wing” Communism: an Infantile Disorder: chapter 7, chapter 8, and chapter 10.
